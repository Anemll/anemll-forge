It explains the idea, how we made it work on the Apple Neural Engine, and what it bought for Qwen3.8-27B on an M6 Mac. 1. The setup in one paragraph

We run Qwen3.8-27B on an M6 Mac with the Neural Engine and the GPU working on the same model (the full article explains that part). It writes its answer with speculative decoding: a small drafter guesses the next 7 tokens, and the big model checks them all in one call of 8 rows (the last confirmed token, called the anchor, plus 7 guesses). Guesses that match what the big model would write are kept; at the first miss the big model supplies the right token and the next round starts. The check costs about the same whether it keeps 1 token or 7, so every kept guess is nearly free speed.

2. The problem: a chain bets everything on one path

With a chain, the 7 guesses form one line. If guess 2 is wrong, guesses 3 to 7 were checked for nothing. The drafter actually knows more than one line: for every position it ranks 16 candidates. Often its second choice is the right one, but a chain has no room for it.

3. The idea: spend the 8 rows on a tree

The drafter suggests 16 token IDs for each of seven possible positions ahead. Starting at the anchor, the selector scores those suggestions for the current parent and puts its four best children on a waiting list. It takes the most probable path on the list, gives its last token a row, and expands from there. A path’s score is the product of its token probabilities. Selection stops after seven proposal nodes. If all seven fall on one line, the ordinary chain verifier handles the cycle.

4. Making the big model check a tree

Three changes let one call check a tree instead of a line:

  • Each row sees only its own branch. A row attends to the cached text, its ancestors and itself. Siblings on other branches are masked out. That is a mask: one 8 x 8 grid per call.
  • Positions follow depth. Two siblings are both "the next word", so they get the same position number.
  • The recurrent layers get a path-aware fix. 48 of the model's 64 layers are Gated DeltaNet layers: instead of looking back over all tokens, they carry a running memory and a short look-back over the last few tokens. In a tree, "the previous token" depends on the branch. We keep the original computation and add one small correction matrix per layer group (we call it corrx0). On plain chains it reproduces the served replies exactly: 45 of 45.

On the M6 the check runs on the Neural Engine, and past 64K tokens part of it (attention over the long history) runs on the GPU between Neural Engine calls; the GPU kernel applies the same mask. When the 7 nodes happen to form a single line, the server skips the tree entirely and runs the ordinary chain check, so confident stretches pay nothing extra.

5. Keeping the winner

After the check, we walk down from the anchor and follow the child the big model agrees with, as far as it goes. Then the winning branch is packed into place as if it had been a chain all along: its rows move to the front slots, along with their recurrent-layer state and their entries in the attention memory. From there the normal chain code takes over, so nothing downstream had to change.

6. Sampling stays honest

Pi samples (temperature 1.0) instead of always taking the top word. For those requests, each node is checked child by child with the standard speculative-sampling rule: accept a child with the probability the big model gives it, else remove it and try the next sibling, else sample from what is left. The output then has the same distribution as plain sampling from the big model. For drafts like ours (the drafter's top candidates, not random samples) we show in the paper that no lossless rule can accept more.

7. Why exactly 8 rows

A bigger tree would keep more guesses, but on the Neural Engine more rows are expensive: in a four-layer probe, 16 rows cost 26 to 39% more per call and 32 rows 87 to 116% more, mostly in the recurrent layers. Timing probes plus replay put the best wider tree only about 1.6% ahead at 8K, and behind at 32K. Eight rows is the sweet spot here.

8. Sliced checks for long chats

The model's attention memory comes in sizes of 8K, 16K, 32K, 64K and 96K tokens, and a check reads the whole size it is in. At 41K tokens of chat, that meant reading 64K. Four extra "sliced" checks at 24K, 40K, 48K and 56K read only the part in use, on the same memory. They matched the full check exactly on 471 tested calls, and over a 19-turn Pi-like session (8.6K to 55.5K tokens) they were 3.4% faster (2.5% without one slow baseline run), with all 152 replies identical.

Small gains need care: two identical restarts of the same server differed by 4.4%, so the paper uses paired runs in both orders and reports where the tree loses (tool-heavy agent turns).

10. What did not help

  • Bigger trees (16 or 32 rows): too expensive on the Neural Engine (section 7).
  • Choosing the tree more cleverly from the same 16 candidates: even the best possible 7 nodes would add only 8.6 to 10.2%, and every cheap rescoring we tried (learned edge scores, token-trigram statistics from Pi's own outputs, one randomly sampled extra branch) lost a little. Bigger gains need a better drafter.
  • Drafting the next round during the check: the drafter needs the big model's internal features of the tokens it just confirmed, which do not exist yet; in replay it kept 48 to 63% fewer tokens.
  • A 16-guess drafter (JetSpec++): more tokens per round, but on this serial schedule it projected 12 to 46% slower overall.
  • Picking tree or chain per round with a cost model: 5.9% slower over a long session.

11. Standing on prior work

Tree verification is not new: SpecInfer, Medusa, EAGLE-2, Sequoia and DDTree built and studied draft trees, and TreeWY, LumoTree and Multi-Future Drafting handle trees for DeltaNet-style hybrid models. Closest on Apple hardware, Mirai Labs' uzu runs DFlash-based trees on the M5 GPU with a deferred DeltaNet state update, much like our packed commit. What this work adds is making it fit and pay off in the fixed 8-row Neural Engine programs of an M6, with the check split across the Neural Engine and the GPU. The paper's What's new section lists each contribution against its closest prior work, and its references give the sources.
